Seasoning is polymerisation, not oiling. Heat drives a very thin layer of oil
past its smoke point until it bonds to the metal as a hard, plastic-like film.
Each round adds another layer. Thin is the whole game, because thick oil pools
and cures sticky rather than hard.

## Equipment

- Carbon steel pan
- High smoke point oil, such as rapeseed or sunflower
- Lint-free cloth or paper towel

## Method

1. Warm the pan on medium heat.
2. Apply a drop of high smoke point oil. Rapeseed or sunflower both work.
3. Wipe it out as though you made a mistake. What stays behind in the metal is
   all you want.
4. Turn the heat to high until the pan smokes, and hold it there about a minute.
5. Drop back to medium.
6. Leave it on the heat for 20 to 30 minutes.
7. Let it cool completely.

Repeat as many rounds as it takes to build a strong polymer and a durable
patina.

## Notes

- Thin layers are everything. A pan that comes out tacky or blotchy had too much
  oil, not too little. Scrub the sticky patch back and redo that round.
- Colour goes straw, then bronze, then brown, then black over successive rounds.
  Even colour matters less than an even film.
- Rapeseed and sunflower are chosen for their smoke points, roughly 200-230°C.
  Olive oil smokes too low and leaves a soft film. Flaxseed cures hardest but
  flakes under real use.
- The first few cooks matter as much as the seasoning. Fatty food, and anything
  browned in oil, keeps building the layer.
- Acid strips seasoning. Keep tomatoes, wine and lemon out of a young pan until
  the patina is properly established.
- Wash with hot water and a brush, dry on the hob, and wipe a trace of oil in
  while still warm. Soap is not the enemy modern lore claims, but a soak is.
